About

How the sounds are made, and the research behind them.

Drops on water

A raindrop hitting a puddle traps a tiny air bubble that rings at the Minnaert frequency, about 3.3 kHz divided by its radius in mm, and rises in pitch as it settles. That chirp is the "plink" in real rain.

Thunder timing

Sound covers a kilometre in about 2.9 seconds. Each strike flashes on the screen, then the rumble arrives after the matching delay, duller and longer the farther away it is.

Tuned to real recordings

Every sound is synthesized, never sampled. Birds sing through a physical model of the songbird syrinx, thunder comes from a model of a lightning channel, and each sound was tuned against real field recordings using two AI listeners and the statistics the human ear uses to recognise natural textures.

The research behind the modes and practices

Sound
  • Nature sounds speed physiological recovery from stress compared with traffic noise, and turn attention outward rather than inward. Alvarsson, Wiens & Nilsson, 2010; Gould van Praag et al., 2017
  • Gentle, effortless interest ("soft fascination") lets tired directed attention recover, hence the slow drift of the weather in Relax mode. Kaplan, 1995
  • Birdsong is among the sounds people find most restorative, and listening to it lowered anxiety in a controlled study. Ratcliffe, Gatersleben & Sowden, 2013; Stobbe et al., Scientific Reports, 2022
  • Across studies, water sounds lifted mood most and birdsong eased stress and annoyance most. Buxton et al., PNAS, 2021
  • Nature sounds played to patients in a cardiac care unit lowered their anxiety compared with usual care. Farokhnezhad Afshar et al., 2016
  • Ocean sounds played through the night after heart surgery improved how well patients said they slept. Williamson, American Journal of Critical Care, 1992
  • Steady pink noise through the night made sleep more stable in a small study, and pink-noise pulses timed to slow waves deepened them in another; the Pink noise soundscape is that sound, kept quiet. Zhou et al., 2012; Papalambros et al., 2017
  • Sudden noise events that rise above the background, more than steady sound, are what wake sleepers. The startle guard caps how far any event can rise. Basner et al., 2011; WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines, 2018
  • Background speech is the most disruptive sound for reading and memory; a steady masking sound reduces it. Banbury et al., 2001
  • Sound with a narrow notch cut around the pitch of the tinnitus reduced its loudness over months in a first trial; later trials were mixed, so treat the Noise sound’s tinnitus notch as comfort to try, not a treatment. Okamoto et al., PNAS, 2010; Stein et al., 2016
  • Generated, non-repeating musical tones ("fractal tones") reduced tinnitus distress in trials with hearing-aid users; the improvising instruments here are tones of that kind, best kept quiet. Sweetow & Sabes, 2010; Herzfeld et al., 2014
  • After a minute of noise centred on the pitch of the tinnitus, about three in four people with tinnitus hear it quieter or gone for seconds to minutes ("residual inhibition"); the Tinnitus shelf’s quiet test lets you try it. The effect passes and is not a treatment. Roberts et al., JARO, 2008; Hu et al., Trends in Hearing, 2021
  • A heartbeat-like rhythm slower than one’s own, felt as a gentle vibration, lowered anxiety before a stressful task; the Resting pulse offers that rhythm as sound. Azevedo et al., Scientific Reports, 2017
  • Viewing a Japanese garden lowered heart rate and lifted mood in older adults, compared with other garden designs; the bamboo fountain brings one of its sounds. Goto et al., HERD, 2013
Breathing
  • Slow breathing near 6 breaths a minute raises heart-rate variability and lowers anxiety. Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014; Zaccaro et al., 2018
  • Five minutes a day of cyclic sighing improved mood and slowed resting breathing more than mindfulness meditation. Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine, 2023
  • For a hard moment, Calm now starts this in one tap: the waves to breathe with, the cyclic sigh and the full screen. A long, slow out-breath is the quickest lever on the heart: it slows as you breathe out and quickens as you breathe in, so breathing led by the out-breath calms within minutes. Russo, Santarelli & O’Rourke, Breathe, 2017; Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine, 2023
  • People tend to breathe along with a slow rhythm they hear, which is why the sound itself swells and settles with the pacer and ocean waves break on each in-breath. Bernardi et al., Heart, 2006
Practices
  • Attention training (focusing on one sound, switching between sounds, then hearing them all at once) reduced worry, anxiety and low mood across clinical and non-clinical studies. Wells, 1990; Knowles, Foden, El-Deredy & Wells, 2016
  • Mindfulness-based programmes lowered tinnitus distress; the Attention, Leaves and Body scan practices are of that kind. Cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence for tinnitus and is a therapist’s work, not an app’s. Gans et al., 2014; Cima et al., 2012
  • Letting thoughts pass, as in "leaves on a stream", is a core exercise of acceptance and commitment therapy, which helps with anxiety and depression in controlled trials. Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson, 1999; A-Tjak et al., 2015
  • Relaxation training, progressive muscle relaxation included, reliably lowers anxiety. Jacobson, 1938; Manzoni et al., BMC Psychiatry, 2008
  • Writing a specific to-do list for five minutes at bedtime helped people fall asleep faster in a sleep-lab study. Scullin et al., Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2018
  • Picturing a stream of unrelated, harmless objects ("cognitive shuffling") is proposed to ease the slide into sleep by crowding out worry; the evidence is still early. Beaudoin, 2013
  • Postponing worries to a set “worry time” each day cut the time spent worrying, and in high worriers it also eased anxiety and sleep problems. Borkovec et al., 1983; McGowan & Behar, 2013
  • Putting a feeling into a word or two (“affect labelling”) lowered the brain’s alarm response to upsetting images, and labelling fear during exposure improved its outcome. Lieberman et al., 2007; Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske, 2012; Torre & Lieberman, 2018
  • Writing three things that went well, and why, each night for a week raised happiness and lowered low mood for six months; gratitude also predicts better sleep through calmer pre-sleep thoughts. Seligman et al., 2005; Wood et al., 2009
  • A body scan (attention moving slowly through the body) raised rest-and-digest activity of the nervous system more than muscle relaxation or quiet waiting did. Ditto, Eclache & Goldman, 2006
  • Five minutes of humming on long out-breaths (the bee breath) lowered heart rate and blood pressure and raised heart-rate variability straight afterwards in healthy adults. Kuppusamy et al., 2018
  • A short self-compassion practice (naming the difficulty, remembering that others feel it too, a kind word to yourself) made the body’s stress response to a social test smaller: less salivary alpha-amylase, a steadier heart and less anxiety. Arch et al., Psychoneuroendocrinology, 2014
  • Twenty minutes of guided imagery relaxed people as much as muscle relaxation did, raising self-rated relaxation and steadily lowering skin conductance and heart rate; deep breathing alone first raised arousal before it settled. Toussaint et al., Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 2021
  • Rating tension from 0 to 10 before and after practice comes from behaviour therapy and helps you notice what works for you. Wolpe, 1969
Music
  • Melodies feel natural when they move mostly by step, turn back after a leap and come to rest on the notes of the key; the improviser is built on these findings, so its phrases are easy to follow and let go of. Narmour, 1990; Huron, 2006
  • Players shape each phrase: it grows until near its end and lands on its last note, and a piece slows into its close the way a runner slows to a stop, the timing listeners preferred. The improviser plays this way, and now and then leans on the note above before coming to rest: the small falling sigh that listeners most often linked with being moved to tears. Friberg & Sundberg, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 1999; Sloboda, Psychology of Music, 1991
  • The harp is the instrument hospitals have tried most: twenty minutes of live harp after surgery lowered patients’ anxiety and pain ratings, and ten minutes of improvised harp in intensive care lowered pain ratings. The harp here plays the same way, improvised and unhurried. Aragon et al., Alternative Therapies in Health and Medicine, 2002; Chiasson et al., Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 2013
  • With a timer set, the music eases as the minutes run down: a little slower, softer and sparser, with longer rests. Music therapists call this the iso principle, meeting the listener where they are and then leading gently toward rest; slower tempo and pauses are what slow the breath and heart. Altshuler, 1948; Heiderscheit & Madson, Music Therapy Perspectives, 2015; Bernardi et al., Heart, 2006
  • A bowed line can sing: performers and the voice carry emotion with the same acoustic cues, a review of 145 studies found, so an instrument that swells, joins its notes and sways in pitch can speak as gently as a voice. The cello here is bowed in the model, note by note. Juslin & Laukka, Psychological Bulletin, 2003
  • Live instrumental music in a hospital ward lowers anxiety: patients who heard live classical music during their dialysis sessions scored lower on anxiety and depression after four weeks than those treated as usual. Serrano Soliva et al., PLOS ONE, 2024
  • Across 104 trials, music lowered stress in body and mind, and the effect on the body was larger when the music was slow, around 60 to 80 beats a minute; the piano and the other instruments here keep to that pace by default, and the Tempo control stays near it. de Witte et al., Health Psychology Review, 2020
  • A sound whose loudness wobbles is felt to fluctuate most strongly at about four wobbles a second, and much less at one or two (fluctuation strength). The vibraphone's motor therefore turns slowly here, near two a second, keeping the shimmer without the warble; the Motor control can stop it or speed it up. Fastl & Zwicker, Psychoacoustics: Facts and Models, 2007
  • Lullabies relax babies: unfamiliar lullabies lowered infants’ heart rate, pupil size and skin conductance more than other songs did. Bainbridge et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2021
  • Across 13 trials with about a thousand adults with insomnia, music at bedtime improved how well people felt they slept; sleep measured by instruments did not clearly change. Jespersen et al., Cochrane Review, 2022
  • Instruments played together now make music together: the first one in sets the key, the others join it on the same chords and beat, and they take turns with the melody, a pair of phrases each, while the others accompany. Two keys at once give notes a semitone apart, which the ear hears as rough; and the more dissonant the same music was made, the less pleasant listeners found it and the more it engaged the brain’s emotion regions the way unpleasant feelings do. Plomp & Levelt, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 1965; Blood, Zatorre, Bermudez & Evans, Nature Neuroscience, 1999
Light
  • Each soundscape's light takes the colour of its place: green for woods and birds, amber for a fire, violet for music, jade for bells. Colours are felt mostly through their brightness and saturation: brighter, softer colours are rated more pleasant and saturated ones more arousing, so the light stays soft and slow and never flashes. Valdez & Mehrabian, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 1994
  • Bright light in the evening, from screens most of all, delays the body clock and the evening rise of melatonin and makes it harder to fall asleep. In Sleep mode the page turns to Night and its light dims to a faint glow. Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler, PNAS, 2015
  • These are self-help practices. If anxiety is severe or does not ease, please talk to a doctor or therapist.
  • Not included: binaural beats. Studies disagree on whether they do anything beyond what relaxing sound already does.
